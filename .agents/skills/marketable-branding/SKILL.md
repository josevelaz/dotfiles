# Marketable Branding

This skill helps turn vague product ideas into branding that is memorable, differentiated, and sellable.

The default stance is:

- optimize for startup and product fit
- generate a full branding pack unless the user asks for a narrower deliverable
- favor bold, category-breaking directions that still feel commercially usable

The goal is not to make the brand sound creative. The goal is to make it easier to understand, want, remember, and choose.

## When To Use

Use this skill when the user wants help with any of the following:

- branding a startup, company, product, app, feature, or AI tool
- finding a stronger brand angle or sharper market position
- generating brand names, taglines, slogans, or messaging
- defining brand voice, personality, or verbal identity
- exploring visual directions for a new brand or rebrand
- making a concept feel more premium, differentiated, credible, or desirable

## Core Principles

- Marketability over novelty. A brand can be unusual, but it still needs to be understandable and easy to back.
- Distinctiveness over category wallpaper. Avoid safe branding that sounds interchangeable with ten funded startups.
- Strategy before surface. Names, taglines, and visuals should come from a strong point of view, not random aesthetics.
- Concrete beats abstract. Prefer clear promises, vivid associations, and strong mental images over airy brand language.
- Recommendation beats option dumping. Lead with the best direction and explain why it wins.

## Default Workflow

### 1. Frame the branding problem

First determine what the brand needs to accomplish.

If the request is underspecified, ask a compact batch of focused questions before ideating. Prefer multiple choice where possible. Usually gather:

- what the product or company actually does
- who the target customer is
- what market or category it sits in
- what makes it meaningfully different
- what feeling or perception the brand should create
- any hard constraints: domain style, naming rules, taboo words, premium vs mass, playful vs serious, B2B vs consumer, geographic or legal constraints

Do not interrogate the user endlessly. If enough signal exists, proceed.

### 2. Find the market tension

Before naming or styling anything, identify:

- the category default the brand should reject
- the customer frustration, aspiration, or identity tension the brand can speak to
- the sharpest promise the brand can credibly own
- the dimension of difference that matters commercially: trust, speed, taste, status, clarity, power, simplicity, rebellion, precision, care, etc.

Boil this into a concise strategic core before expanding.

### 3. Generate brand territories

Produce 3 distinct territories when the user wants exploration. Each territory should feel like a coherent commercial angle, not just a mood board title.

For each territory, include:

- territory name
- one-sentence idea
- who it attracts
- why it can win in-market
- risk or tradeoff

Lead with the recommended territory.

The recommended territory should usually be bold enough to stand apart, but grounded enough that a founder could actually pitch, market, and design around it.

### 4. Build the branding pack

Unless the user asks for something smaller, generate a full branding pack with these sections:

#### Brand core

- brand thesis
- positioning statement
- target audience summary
- value proposition
- differentiators
- brand personality traits

#### Naming directions

- 3 naming routes with a short rationale for each
- 5-7 names per route
- flag the strongest 2-3 names overall

Names should be evaluated for memorability, pronunciation, distinctiveness, emotional fit, and commercial plausibility.

Do not imply trademark or domain clearance unless it was explicitly checked. If relevant, state that legal and domain validation still need a real follow-up check.

#### Taglines and messaging

- 5-10 taglines or headline directions
- 3 messaging pillars
- proof points or reasons to believe
- a short brand story or launch narrative

#### Voice and verbal identity

- voice attributes
- words to use
- words to avoid
- example lines in the brand voice

#### Visual direction

- creative direction title
- color and contrast direction
- typography mood
- imagery or illustration style
- motion or interaction feel if relevant
- references to the kind of world the brand should evoke

Keep visual direction strategic and vivid. Do not default to generic tech gradients or interchangeable startup minimalism unless the brief truly calls for it.

### 5. Apply the commercial filter

Before finalizing, pressure-test the outputs.

Cull or rewrite ideas that fail on any of these:

- too generic to own
- too obscure to understand
- too weird to trust
- too descriptive to feel premium
- too trend-driven to last
- too similar to common startup naming or messaging patterns

If several directions are good, say which one you would actually bet on.

### 6. Present with conviction

Structure the response so the user can make decisions quickly.

Prefer this order:

1. recommended direction and why
2. alternate directions worth considering
3. the best names and taglines
4. the final brand system pieces
5. next decisions the user should make

Do not hand the user a giant undifferentiated brainstorm. Curate.

## Quality Bar

Great outputs from this skill should feel:

- strategically sharp
- commercially aware
- memorable on first read
- easy to imagine in a real launch
- distinct from category cliches
- internally coherent across name, message, and visual direction

## Anti-Patterns To Avoid

- vague praise words with no strategic edge: `innovative`, `seamless`, `empowering`, `smart`, `future-ready`
- empty startup phrasing: `the future of`, `platform for`, `reimagining`, `next-gen`, `AI-powered` unless strategically necessary
- names that are hard to say, hard to spell, or feel algorithmically generated
- brand systems where the messaging says one thing and the visual direction suggests another
- a pile of options with no recommendation or editorial point of view
- boldness for its own sake when it harms trust or clarity

## Output Modes

Adapt the depth to the user's ask.

- If the user asks for a quick pass, give 1 strong direction with a compact branding pack.
- If the user asks for exploration, give 3 territories and clearly mark the winner.
- If the user asks only for names, still anchor them in positioning before listing names.
- If the user asks only for messaging or visuals, briefly restate the underlying brand angle first.

## Suggested Response Shape

Use a structure close to this when helpful:

```text
Recommended Direction
- one paragraph on the strongest brand angle

Brand Core
- positioning
- audience
- promise
- differentiators

Naming Routes
- route 1 + names
- route 2 + names
- route 3 + names

Best Picks
- top names with reasons

Taglines
- strongest lines

Voice
- traits, use, avoid, example lines

Visual Direction
- clear art direction guidance

What I Would Choose
- the single strongest route and why
```

## Example Triggers

- "Help me brand this startup so it feels premium and hard to ignore."
- "Come up with names and taglines for an AI recruiting product."
- "I need a bold but marketable brand direction for a fintech app."
- "Rebrand this B2B SaaS so it stops sounding generic."
- "Give me positioning, naming, voice, and visual direction for a new consumer app."

## Final Reminder

Always aim for branding that a founder could actually ship: clear enough to sell, distinctive enough to matter, and bold enough to be remembered.
